# Voice

Read this before drafting. It describes the target. The detector's checklist is `review/cold-audit.md` and belongs to auditors, not to a drafting context.

## How human prose is shaped

Importance is unevenly distributed. Some sentences carry enormous weight; others are connective tissue or a half-callback to something three paragraphs back. Paragraphs vary in length and weight, two ideas share a paragraph when they belong together, and a point lands once, in the place it lands hardest, trusting the reader to carry it forward. A piece where every paragraph is a self-contained summary of equal size reads like a deck of index cards; a piece with real turbulence reads like a person thinking.

## Camera on the subject

Every sentence carries content about the world. The text never refers to itself, its sections, its structure, or what it is about to do; if a sentence would survive being moved to a different document about a different subject, it is scaffolding, and scaffolding comes down before the reader arrives. Candor reads as candor only when it arrives unannounced. Make the ask, state the hard thing, land the transition, all without commentary on the fact that you are doing so. After the ask, one sentence of shape ("a working session, with whoever you'd want on it") is the whole move; end on the stakes or something concrete.

## Plain machinery

Say "is." The plain copula is the strongest verb in expository prose: "the program is the largest of its kind," "X coined it." Reach for a more muscular verb only when it adds information about how.

Pick a word and reuse it. A writer confident that "zone" is the word uses "zone" every time.

Attribute claims to named people and named institutions. Where you would gesture at a consensus, find the person who said it or make the claim in your own voice and own it.

Count your points honestly: two ideas make two items, four make four.

Punctuate with commas, periods, colons, and the occasional semicolon; parentheses carry the rare true aside. When a sentence wants heavier machinery, split it or subordinate the clause. Straight quotes. Headings are sentence case and state what the section contains ("What the cache keeps, and what a write can invalidate"). Emphasis comes from sentence position and word choice; bold marks the single rule the reader must be able to find again.

## Audience gloss

At first mention of an event, case, person, or precedent, assume the reader has never heard of it and give a one-clause setup: "the 2019 outage, a failover bug the vendor later fixed in full." If you cannot spare the clause, cut the example. Gloss any term that needs more than three words to explain, inline, on first use. Inform, never assign: "a question for the platform team is how it sets the threshold" respects a reader senior to or independent of you.

## Truth over ornament

Each sentence is the truest version of what you mean. Open with the first true declarative sentence you can write. Where a contrast is real, assert the true thing and let the false thing fall away unstated. Concede in the same breath you claim: "the figure counts dollars recovered, not claims paid, so it overstates how often the program comes out whole."

Given two passages with nearly the same meaning, the shorter one is better. In sourced or forensic writing the qualifier is often the finding; tighten how a caveat is phrased and keep the caveat.

## Soul

Clean prose with nobody home reads as machine output. A human writer has opinions, varied rhythm (short sentences, then a long one that takes its time), acknowledged complexity, first person where it fits, and a little mess. Soul is voice, never invented substance: opinions and emphasis are yours to add; facts trace to the source material or the author's own knowledge. Trust the reader to feel what the writing earns; the sentence after the strong one, explaining what the reader just felt, is the one to delete.

## Tone by situation

Internal memo: frank, expressive, wit deployed sparingly so it lands as a surprise. External memo: warm and composed. To a vendor's or customer's technical team about a system they own: give the finding and how it was checked, hedge once in the first person, and stop; no remedy steps or side asks unless they asked, and close on availability. To a lawyer or other expert who drafted the documents: lead each hard item with a one-line plain conclusion, nest the cites under it, hedge the conclusion in the first person ("that reads to me like," "in case I'm wrong"), and stop at the finding. Persuasive memo: tight, grounded in citation, conceding small points to strengthen the large one. Use the word that maps most precisely onto the concept regardless of syllable count; simplify structure for accessibility, never vocabulary. Contract by ear: narrative prose and anything spoken aloud takes contractions where a voice would; verbatim quotes, formal on-the-record text, and deliberate parallels keep full forms.

## Whose voice

**The owner's.** When the owner has samples of their own writing (sent emails, past posts, edits they made to earlier drafts), read them until the writer is audible, then write. The grain to listen for: how directly they ask, where the warmth sits (often the open and close), how they handle numbers and names, whether they argue in first person, how short their verdicts are, how they close a scheduling note. Samples beat a description of the voice. Keep them in a samples file the owner controls, and keep any "befores" and commentary separate, for auditors only.

**House** (unsigned, staff-facing, or no samples available): plain subject labels for headings, guided-tour structure ("To set the stage, assume..."), mechanics glossed in plain language before the argument leans on them, caveats woven in place rather than gathered into a set piece.
