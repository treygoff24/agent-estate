# Shape: raw material into an article, paragraph by paragraph

The author passes a markdown file of raw material: a list of fragments, a wall of prose, a transcript. Read it end to end before anything else. Then run a shaping session that produces a separate article document. The exploring is done and the pile is fixed: commit to a structure and mine the pile to fill it. The raw file is read-only here. If the author did not say where to save the article, ask once and remember.

## The loop

1. **Read the pile** in full and form a sense of what is in it.
2. **Establish the prerequisites.** Settle with him what the reader knows walking in: the concepts grounded from the start. Everything else must be grounded by a block before a later block leans on it.
3. **Draft two or three candidate openings.** Each implies a different thesis or angle. Show all of them; make him pick or compose a hybrid. The chosen opening defines what the rest must do.
4. **Grow paragraph by paragraph.** After the opening lands, ask "given this opening, what does the reader need to hear next?" and pull material from the pile to answer. The next block may lean only on grounded concepts, and grounds new ones as it lands. Argue about the form each block takes (paragraph, list, table, callout, quote) and make the choice defensible.
5. **Append to the article file as you go**, each agreed block immediately, so he sees it taking shape.
6. **Loop step 4 until done.** He decides when it is done.

## Grounding

Every concept is grounded before a block leans on it: the reader brought it (a prerequisite) or an earlier block introduced it. The unit is the concept, not the word; a block can lean on an idea the reader lacks with no jargon in sight. Where a concept has a name, grounding it means landing the idea and the term together. Keep a running list of what is grounded. When the next move needs an ungrounded concept, grounding it is the next move. The lever is what you make a prerequisite versus what you ground inside the piece: demand too much up front and you shut readers out; ground too much inside and the opening drowns in definitions.

## The conversation

A grilling session inverted: "what is this piece actually arguing, and in what order does the reader need to hear it?" Push back; refuse weak transitions; cut the paragraph that does not earn its place. Moves to keep using: "What does this paragraph do for the reader that the previous one didn't?" "If I cut this, what breaks?" "Is this prose, or should it be a list? Why prose?" "This sentence is doing two jobs: split it or pick one." "The opening promised X; we've drifted to Y. Re-thread it or change the opening."

## Pulling from the pile

The pile is a quarry, never a script. Pull a fragment, rework it to fit the surrounding paragraph, place it; split, merge, paraphrase as the article needs. When the pile lacks something the article needs, name the gap: "we need an example here and the pile doesn't have one; give me one now or we cut this section."

## Format arguments to have out loud

Prose carries argument; lists carry parallel items. Callouts hold tips and asides only when they would derail the argument inline. A shape that repeats three or more times with the same fields is a table. Quote when the wording is the point; paraphrase when only the idea matters.

## Rhythm

Append as each block is agreed; re-read the file from disk before every write; never overwrite blindly; rewrite a paragraph in place when he asks and leave the rest alone. Out of scope: mining new fragments beyond named gaps, editing the raw file, publishing or platform formatting.
