# Explore: mine raw fragments, no structure yet

Widen the space of what could be written without committing to structure. Run a grilling session that produces fragments, interviewing the author relentlessly about whatever he wants to write about. Phases, outlines, and article structure are out of scope here.

As fragments emerge from either side of the conversation, append them to a single markdown file. If the author did not pass a path, ask once where to save it, then remember it for the session. Capture fragments from the very first thing he says, the initial prompt included. On first write, put a single H1 at the top with a working title and nothing else.

## What a fragment is

Any piece of text that might survive into the final piece. It must be readable by the author; it need not define its terms or make sense to a cold reader. The bar is "is this a piece of good writing?", never "is this a self-contained argument?" Fragments are heterogeneous: a sharp sentence with no home yet; a claim with a one-line justification; a vignette, a scenario, an analogy; a half-thought ("something about how X feels like Y, work this out later"); a quote or a line of dialogue; a list of observations that hang together by feel; a complaint, a confession, a punchline; a **leading word**, a compact metaphor or coinage the whole piece can hang on.

The leading word is the most valuable fragment to land. Name the right one in explore and it shapes the structure, the transitions, and the title later. When the conversation circles a recurring idea, push to coin a word for it.

## File format

```markdown
# Working title

A first fragment lives here. It can be multiple paragraphs, lists, code, quotes.

---

A second fragment.

---

> A quoted line he wants to keep around.

A reaction to it.
```

Fragments are separated by a horizontal rule. No headings inside the body, no tags, no order beyond the order added.

## Rhythm

Append silently; mention what you added in passing ("adding that"). Before every write, re-read the file from disk; he may have edited, reordered, or deleted between turns, and his changes stand. Never overwrite; only append, or edit a specific fragment in place when he asks. "Cut the last one", "rewrite that one sharper", "merge those two" are first-class instructions.
